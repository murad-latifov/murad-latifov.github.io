The Impact of Professional Sports Franchise Movements on Urban Crime Rates

While municipalities often justify large public subsidies for professional sports franchises through expected social benefits, the long-term externalities on public safety remain largely underexplored. This paper investigates the long-term effect of professional sports team entries and exits on local urban crime rates. The findings demonstrate that the crime externalities of professional sports are highly sensitive to market saturation. In solo markets, franchise movements generate significant and symmetric shocks. The arrival of a city’s first major franchise structurally increases overall crime, whereas the departure of a sole franchise leads to a proportional decrease. However, in established multi-team mar kets, the marginal addition or loss of a franchise yields no statistically significant changes in crime.

The Effect of Minimum Wage Policies on Reservation Wages

This paper examines how state minimum wage policies shape the lowest wages at which workers are willing to accept employment. Preliminary results indicate that the effect is concentrated among low-income households in states where the minimum wage binds more tightly relative to prevailing local wages, suggesting that statutory floors influence not only realized pay but also the expectations workers bring to the labor market.

The Messi Effect: Dynamic Pricing and Information Shocks in Secondary Ticket Markets

with James Kemper and Lissa De Mesa

This paper analyzes how a major international sporting event — the FIFA World Cup — reshapes prices in secondary ticket markets. Because Lionel Messi played every match through the final, the tournament provides an unusually clean setting in which to observe how the premium attached to a superstar evolves in response to on-field performance, and how quickly that new information is capitalized into resale prices.
